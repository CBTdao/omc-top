What is outcome-based pricing (and who gets to define the outcome)?

Plain-English definition · Updated 2026-11-23. Primary sources: Intercom's official pricing page and Zendesk's official pricing FAQ (both checked 2026-11-23), plus Salesforce's official Agentforce pricing page and rate card (checked 2026-11-22).

AITop is an independent guide. Prices and features change fast — always check the vendor's page.

The 30-second answer

Outcome-based pricing is billing an AI agent per successfully resolved request instead of per seat or per token. The category leader sets the anchor: Intercom lists its Fin AI Agent at $0.99 per outcome on its official pricing page, with a hard limit of one outcome per conversation. Zendesk bills the same idea under a different name — an automated resolution, which its official FAQ defines as a request resolved by the AI without escalation to a human agent. Salesforce sells consumption two ways: Flex Credits metered per action (its rate card is $500 per 100,000 credits) or a flat per-conversation price that charges whether or not the conversation was resolved. This is the one pricing model where the meter is a definition: the vendor writes what "resolved" means, and that sentence is worth more than the sticker price.

What does outcome-based pricing actually mean?

Outcome-based pricing is a billing model in which the customer pays for a result rather than for access. You are not buying a seat that a human occupies monthly, and you are not buying tokens metered per API call; you are buying one successfully resolved request, counted once, priced at a published rate. If the AI fails and a human takes over, the outcome does not bill — which is the model's entire sales argument: the vendor only earns when the AI actually wins.

The mechanic only works because AI support agents produce a countable event. A human ticket queue produces "tickets closed", which is a measure of effort; an AI agent produces "conversations the model resolved end-to-end without a handoff", which is a measure of output. The billing unit therefore has to be defined in the contract, not measured from physics — and every vendor's definition differs slightly. That is not a detail. It is the product.

Three unit shapes dominate in 2026. Per outcome: Intercom's Fin AI Agent, billed at a flat rate for each conversation the agent resolves, with one outcome charged per conversation at most. Per automated resolution: Zendesk's unit, restricted by definition to requests the AI closes without escalation. Per action or per conversation: Salesforce's consumption model, where Flex Credits meter the individual steps an agent takes, and a per-conversation price charges for the conversation itself whether or not it ended in a resolution.

What does outcome-based pricing cost in 2026?

Intercom Fin: $0.99 per outcome, one per conversation

Intercom's official pricing page states $0.99 per outcome across plans, and states the cap explicitly: "One outcome per conversation, max." That single sentence does a lot of work. It means a long, multi-turn conversation that eventually resolves bills once — good for the buyer — and that a conversation which resolves two separate problems still bills once, which caps the vendor's revenue per thread. The rate is charged on top of whatever you pay for seats, because Fin sits inside Intercom's own helpdesk plans.

Zendesk: the unit is an automated resolution

Zendesk's official pricing FAQ says AI agents are included in every Suite and Support plan, with pricing based on the successful outcomes they deliver, and defines the meter this way: automated resolutions "are the unit of measurement used for calculating and billing your account for AI agent usage", and paying per automated resolution means you "pay only for customer requests that were successfully resolved by the AI agent, without any escalation to a human agent." Note what the definition excludes: the moment a human is pulled in, the event does not count. That is a stricter test than "the customer seemed satisfied", and it pushes the risk of a bad handoff onto the vendor.

Salesforce: credits per action, or a conversation price

Salesforce's official Agentforce pricing page offers consumption-based pricing, with Flex Credits or Conversations, or per-user licensing. Flex Credits bill the individual actions an agent performs — the published rate card is $500 per 100,000 credits, and a typical resolution that fires a handful of actions costs a fraction of a dollar in credits. Conversations bill the conversation itself, resolved or not. On the same vendor's rate card that is a wide spread: at roughly $0.10 per action and three actions per resolution, a resolution lands near $0.30; a $2.00 per-conversation charge on a 60% resolution rate lands at $3.33 per resolution (our arithmetic on Salesforce's published rates).

Why does it matter when you're choosing a tool?

Because a per-resolution price and a per-conversation price are not comparable numbers, and the difference can be 5–10× on the same workload. The only honest comparison is cost per contact, and it takes two divisions. A $0.99 per-outcome price at a 70% resolution rate costs about $1.41 per contact: you pay $0.99 for the 70% that resolve and $0 for the 30% that escalate, so the bill per 100 contacts is $69.30 and the contacts you actually served number 100. A $2.00 per-conversation price at a 60% resolution rate costs $3.33 per resolution: 100 conversations cost $200, and only 60 of them ended in a resolution. Same workload shape, 2.4× the effective price — and the cheaper-looking sticker can be the more expensive contract.

The second reason it matters is that the definition is the deliverable. "Resolved" can mean the customer confirmed it, the customer stopped replying, the ticket was closed by automation, or the agent completed a defined workflow. Each of those is a different product at the same price. Buyer guides published by independent consultancies in 2026 describe the same dispute across vendors: a conversation a customer abandons after reading an answer bills as resolved, because the absence of a follow-up is treated as agreement. Whether that is fair depends entirely on your traffic — for a password-reset queue, silence really is success; for a billing dispute, silence usually means the customer gave up and called.

The third reason: outcome pricing moves the risk of a bad answer onto the vendor's invoice, and vendors price that risk in. Compare the per-resolution rate with the underlying model cost. A support resolution reads perhaps 15,000–30,000 tokens of retrieved documentation and writes a few thousand back; at current cheap-model rates that is cents, not dollars. The gap between cents and $0.99 is what you pay for the workflow, the integrations, the audit trail, and the fact that a failed resolution is free. The honest framing: you are not buying tokens, you are buying someone else's accountability for the outcome — and that is a reasonable thing to buy, as long as you know that is what the invoice says.

What is true in 2026 specifically?

Is per-resolution always cheaper than per-seat?

No — and that is the reverse of the marketing. Per-resolution saves money when volume is low and resolution rates are high; per-seat saves money when volume is high and stable, because a seat is a fixed cost that a heavy queue amortises. The break-even is not a price comparison but a volume line: below it, you want to pay per outcome; above it, the per-outcome meter becomes a variable cost that grows with success. Run your own numbers on monthly resolved volume, not on the vendor's comparison chart.

What happens when the AI resolves nothing?

You pay the platform fee and nothing per outcome — which sounds like free insurance until you notice the second-order cost: every escalated conversation still consumes a human seat, so a low resolution rate means you are paying for both the AI platform and the full human queue. The metric to negotiate is therefore not the per-resolution price but the resolution rate floor, and the clause to read is what happens to the rate in month three, after the pilot's easy tickets are gone.

How do you audit an outcome bill?

Ask for the event log, not the summary. You want one row per billed outcome with a conversation ID, the definition that triggered the charge (customer-confirmed, auto-closed, workflow-completed), the timestamp, and whether a human ever touched the thread. A vendor that cannot produce that export is charging you for a number you cannot verify — and in a model where the meter is a definition, an unauditable bill is the whole risk.

Quick checklist

  • Convert every quote to cost per contact before comparing. Price ÷ resolution rate. $0.99 at 70% is $1.41 per contact; $2.00 per conversation at 60% is $3.33 per resolution.
  • Get the definition of "resolved" in writing. Customer-confirmed, silence-after-answer, auto-close and workflow-completed are four different products at one price.
  • Check the cap. Intercom's published cap is one outcome per conversation — a long thread that solves two problems bills once. Ask every vendor for their equivalent sentence.
  • Confirm what does not bill. Escalations to a human, greetings, and abandoned chats should be free; if any of those bill, the effective price is higher than the sticker.
  • Negotiate the resolution-rate floor, not just the rate. A cheap per-resolution price with a collapsing resolution rate is a seat contract with extra steps.
  • Demand the event export. One row per billed outcome, with the trigger definition and a conversation ID. No export, no audit.

The bottom line

Outcome-based pricing means paying per successfully resolved request instead of per seat or per token — and in 2026 the category is defined by three incompatible meters. Intercom charges $0.99 per outcome with a published cap of one outcome per conversation; Zendesk bills automated resolutions, defined as resolved with no human escalation; Salesforce meters actions through Flex Credits ($500 per 100,000 credits) or charges per conversation whether or not it resolved. Because the unit is a definition, the only comparable number is cost per contact: $0.99 at a 70% resolution rate is about $1.41, while $2.00 per conversation at 60% is about $3.33 per resolution. Summary: outcome pricing shifts risk to the vendor, but the vendor writes the definition of the outcome — so negotiate the definition and the resolution-rate floor before you negotiate the rate.
