What are commercial-use rights for AI music?

Plain-English definition · Updated 2026-10-06. Numbers dated; verify with the vendor.

The 30-second answer

Commercial-use rights are the vendor's permission to publish, sell, sync or monetize the music you generated — and in 2026 they are the single most confusing line in AI music pricing. The pattern across every major tool: making songs is free or cheap, but keeping and releasing them is gated to paid tiers, metered by download caps (Suno Pro: about 20 downloads a month), and complicated further because "commercial use" and "streaming distribution" are often different rights. And the fine print nobody reads: a license to use is not a copyright — purely AI-generated music generally can't be copyrighted at all in the US.

What it actually means

There are three separate questions hiding in "can I use this song?", and vendors answer them on different rows of the pricing page. Can I download it? — a storage question, metered hard: Suno's free tier grants no commercial rights and paid tiers cap downloads (roughly 20/month on Pro, 60 on Premier, extras sold separately). Can I monetize it? — the commercial-rights question, tier-gated. Can I put it on Spotify? — the distribution question, which is narrower still: ElevenLabs requires Creator or above for streaming distribution, and some services disallow streaming distribution on any plan.

The trap that catches beginners: credits and downloads are different counters. "Unlimited generations" and "500 tracks a month" both describe making music. What you keep is the download row — read that row first, always.

Why it matters when you're picking a tool

Because the tool that generates the best demo may be the worst one to release through. The categories in our 2026 ranking are separated less by sound quality than by rights mechanics: Suno's paid downloads carry commercial rights behind the cap; ElevenLabs ties distribution scope to plan tier; Mureka bundles full commercial rights into paid plans; and Udio — still praised for fidelity on stale lists — has allowed no exports at all since 29 October 2025, meaning a finished Udio track cannot leave the app to be released anywhere. A tool whose output cannot exit is not a production tool, whatever it sounds like.

The 2026 reality check

Two facts frame the whole category. First, the copyright gap: the US Copyright Office has held that purely AI-generated output lacks human authorship and cannot be copyrighted — so your commercial license is a shield the vendor hands you, not property you own, and it lasts only as long as the terms and your subscription record support it. Second, the litigation cloud: the labels sued the major generators, and the fight continued into 2026 (new claims over Suno's V6 were filed by Sony and Universal in September 2026). That doesn't invalidate paid-plan licenses — vendors indemnify differently, and most creators' releases are unaffected — but it means terms can change with a court ruling, and the tools most at risk of a forced pivot are the ones already restricting exports. Check the date on any comparison article you read; this market rewrites itself quarterly.

Quick checklist

  • Read the download row, not the credits row — that's what you actually keep.
  • Confirm the tier's rights in writing: personal, commercial, or commercial + streaming distribution are three different grants.
  • Keep subscription records for the period each track was generated — your license proof lives there.
  • Assume no copyright protection: anyone can reuse your purely AI track, and you can't sue them for it.

Where you'll hit it

This term is the spine of the best AI music generators of 2026 — download caps and distribution tiers are the real ranking criteria — and it follows every track you feed into AI video tools as background music. Related terms: stem separation, voice cloning — or back to the full glossary.

The bottom line

In AI music, the license is the product. Sound quality is table stakes; what separates a usable tool from an expensive toy is whether the file can leave the app, on what tier, with what rights, provable when it matters. Decide what "release" means for you, find the row that grants it, and keep the receipts — the rest is genre taste.
